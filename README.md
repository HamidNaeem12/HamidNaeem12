### `01 — THE PROBLEM I WORK ON`

The EU AI Act, the US executive frameworks and the OECD principles have one thing in common: they were drafted by people who assumed they would also be the ones applying them. Pakistan — and most of the world — applies them anyway, without the regulators, registries or litigation capacity the text quietly presumes.

I come at that gap two ways. **Writing**, on what breaks when a framework crosses a border. **Building**, the tools that let a practitioner apply one named statute faster than they could by hand. On paper, two careers. In practice, one habit — reading a system until you find where it breaks — pointed at two different rulebooks. The writing produces positions; the code proves they're implementable. Neither is much use alone.

### `03 — THE POSITION`

> A regulation is adequate only if the state applying it can staff it, fund it, and litigate it.

Call it **Contextual Adequacy.** By that test, most transplanted AI governance fails — and it fails on capacity, not on substance. The drafting assumed an enforcement apparatus that exists in Brussels and Washington and nowhere else. The novel / non-novel capability line, in particular, is unusable for a state that had no part in drawing it: it asks a regulator to measure distance from a frontier it was never standing on.

That argument is what *Regulating Intelligence at the Periphery* is for. If you are writing on the same problem, we should be talking.

### `05 — SCOPE`

*Objection: isn't a chatbot answering legal questions dangerous?* Sustained, partially.

Every tool above is scoped to one named statute with one named source. None of it is legal advice, a legal opinion, or a substitute for counsel licensed in your jurisdiction. The tools read the law to you faster; they do not tell you what the law is in your case. The interpretations are mine, offered for argument — which is why each one carries a citation.
